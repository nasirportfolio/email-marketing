How to Create an Email Marketing Campaign Brief

Quick answer: An email marketing campaign brief is a simple planning document that defines what an email campaign needs to accomplish, who it is for, what the audience should understand, what action they should take, and how the campaign will be evaluated.

Writing an email can look simple until you actually sit down to create one. You may know that you want to promote a product, share an update, educate subscribers, or bring inactive customers back. But without a clear plan, the email can become difficult to write.

The message may try to do too many things at once. The call to action may be unclear. The content may not match the audience receiving it.

A campaign brief helps solve that problem before you start writing.

In this guide, you will learn how to create a practical email marketing campaign brief that you can use for newsletters, promotional emails, educational campaigns, product announcements, and other email campaigns.

What Is an Email Marketing Campaign Brief?

An email marketing campaign brief is a short planning document that describes the important decisions behind an email campaign.

Instead of starting with the subject line or the first paragraph, you start by answering basic questions:

  • Why are we sending this email?
  • Who should receive it?
  • What do we want subscribers to understand?
  • What action should they take?
  • What information or offer should the email contain?
  • When should it be sent?
  • How will we measure the campaign?

The brief does not need to be complicated. Its purpose is to create clarity before production begins.

Why Create a Campaign Brief Before Writing?

A campaign brief can make the writing process easier because you make the major decisions before trying to write the email itself.

1. It gives the email one clear purpose

An email can contain several pieces of information, but it should still have a clear primary purpose. A brief forces you to define that purpose.

2. It keeps the audience in focus

An email written for a new subscriber may need a different message from one written for a long-term customer. Defining the audience before writing helps prevent a generic message.

3. It makes the call to action clearer

If you know the desired action before writing, you can build the email around that action instead of adding a CTA as an afterthought.

4. It reduces unnecessary content

A brief gives you a reason to remove information that does not support the campaign objective.

5. It creates a reference for the campaign

If several people are involved in planning, writing, design, or review, the brief gives everyone the same basic direction.

If you are working alone, it can still serve as a checklist before you send the campaign.

What Should an Email Campaign Brief Include?

A useful campaign brief does not need dozens of fields. Start with the information that affects the actual email.

Brief field Question to answer
Campaign name What will you call this campaign internally?
Primary goal What is the main purpose of the email?
Audience Which subscribers should receive it?
Main message What is the most important idea the reader should understand?
Offer or content What are you giving, promoting, or explaining?
Call to action What should the reader do next?
Timing When should the email be sent?
Success measurement Which results will you review?

Step 1: Give the Campaign a Clear Name

Start with a simple internal campaign name.

The name does not need to be creative. It needs to help you identify the campaign later.

Example:
Spring Product Education Campaign

If you send several campaigns each month, consistent naming can also make your planning and reporting easier.

Step 2: Define the Primary Goal

The goal is one of the most important parts of the brief.

Ask yourself: What is the main reason this email exists?

Possible goals include:

  • Educate subscribers about a topic
  • Introduce a product
  • Promote an existing offer
  • Drive traffic to useful content
  • Encourage a specific next step
  • Re-engage inactive subscribers
  • Support a customer communication

Avoid writing a goal such as “get more engagement” without explaining what the campaign is actually intended to accomplish.

A clearer goal might be: “Introduce subscribers to a new product category and encourage interested readers to view the products.”

Step 3: Define the Audience

The same email does not necessarily need to go to your entire list.

Your campaign brief should identify the intended audience.

For example, the audience might be:

  • New subscribers
  • Existing customers
  • Subscribers interested in a particular topic
  • People who previously viewed a product
  • Subscribers who have not engaged recently

The exact segmentation method depends on the data available in your email platform. The important point is to decide who the message is intended for before writing it.

Your broader email marketing customer journey planning can help provide context for this decision.

Step 4: Write the Main Message in One Sentence

Before writing the email, summarize the central message in one sentence.

This is not necessarily the subject line. It is the idea you want the reader to take away.

Weak planning statement:
Tell subscribers about our products.

Clearer planning statement:
Explain how the new product range can help customers solve a specific problem and invite interested readers to explore it.

If you cannot explain the main message simply, the campaign may need more planning before you begin writing.

Step 5: Decide What the Reader Should Do

Every campaign brief should identify the intended next action.

This could be:

  • Read an article
  • Visit a product page
  • View a landing page
  • Register for an event
  • Complete a specific step
  • Reply to the email

A useful question is: If the reader finds this email useful, what should they do next?

Your CTA should support the campaign goal rather than compete with it.

If you need help planning the overall campaign process, see email marketing campaign management.

Step 6: Decide What Content the Email Needs

Once the goal, audience, message, and CTA are clear, decide what information the email actually needs.

Depending on the campaign, that might include:

  • A short introduction
  • The main benefit or explanation
  • Supporting details
  • A product or content link
  • A clear CTA
  • Relevant context the reader needs before taking action

Do not add sections simply because they are available. Each part should support the reader's understanding or the campaign objective.

Step 7: Define the Tone and Writing Direction

Your brief can also establish how the email should sound.

For example:

  • Educational and straightforward
  • Friendly and conversational
  • Concise and product-focused
  • Helpful and instructional
  • Professional and informative

You do not need a long list of adjectives. One or two useful instructions are usually enough.

For example: “Keep the message practical, conversational, and focused on the reader's problem.”

Good planning here also connects with email marketing copywriting.

Step 8: Add the Timing and Sending Context

Record when the campaign is intended to be sent and any important timing considerations.

For example, a brief might specify:

  • Planned send date
  • Approximate send time
  • Campaign duration if applicable
  • Whether a follow-up email is planned

Timing should also fit into your wider content plan. This is where an email marketing content calendar becomes useful.

The campaign brief answers: What are we trying to accomplish with this campaign?

The content calendar answers: Where does this campaign fit into our overall email schedule?

Step 9: Decide How You Will Measure the Campaign

Before sending the email, decide which results you intend to review.

Depending on the campaign, you may examine measures such as:

  • Delivery results
  • Opens where the platform provides them
  • Clicks
  • Click-through rate
  • Conversions
  • Unsubscribes
  • Revenue where applicable

Not every metric needs to be the primary measure for every campaign. The important thing is to connect measurement with the campaign's purpose.

A Simple Email Campaign Brief Example

Here is a hypothetical example of how the completed brief might look.

Field Example
Campaign name New Product Education
Primary goal Introduce the new product category
Audience Subscribers interested in the relevant product category
Main message Explain the problem the new products are designed to address
Content Short explanation, key benefits, and product link
Primary CTA Explore the products
Tone Helpful, concise, and conversational
Timing Planned according to the email content calendar
Measurement Clicks and downstream campaign results

Notice that the brief does not contain the complete email. It simply provides enough direction to make writing and production more focused.

How to Turn the Brief Into an Email

Once the brief is complete, you can use it as the foundation for writing the campaign.

  1. Review the audience and their likely needs.
  2. Write the main message in plain language.
  3. Build the email around that message.
  4. Remove information that does not support the purpose.
  5. Add the primary CTA.
  6. Write and refine the subject line.
  7. Review the email from the subscriber's perspective.
  8. Check links, formatting, personalization, and other campaign settings.

This approach separates planning from writing. That can make the actual writing process much easier.

Common Email Campaign Brief Mistakes

Making the brief too complicated

A brief should create clarity, not become another large project. Start with the fields that affect the campaign directly.

Trying to give the campaign several primary goals

If everything is the main objective, the email can lose focus. Define one primary goal and document secondary objectives only when they are genuinely useful.

Writing the brief after the email

A brief is most useful when it guides the work. Creating it after the campaign has already been written removes much of its planning value.

Ignoring the audience

A well-written email can still be poorly planned if it is sent to people for whom the message is not relevant.

Choosing the CTA too late

The CTA should be considered while planning the campaign, because it affects the structure and message of the email.

Measuring everything without identifying what matters

Collecting data is useful, but the campaign brief should identify which results matter most for the campaign's purpose.

Email Campaign Brief Checklist

  • ☐ Campaign has a clear internal name
  • ☐ Primary goal is defined
  • ☐ Target audience is identified
  • ☐ Main message is clear
  • ☐ Relevant content or offer is defined
  • ☐ Primary CTA is selected
  • ☐ Tone and writing direction are clear
  • ☐ Timing is planned
  • ☐ Measurement approach is defined
  • ☐ Campaign fits the wider email calendar

FAQ

How long should an email marketing campaign brief be?

It can be very short. For many campaigns, one page or a simple structured checklist is enough. The right length depends on the complexity of the campaign and how many people are involved.

Do I need a campaign brief for every email?

Not every simple email requires a formal document. However, having a brief or brief-style checklist can be useful for campaigns involving promotions, multiple stakeholders, segmentation, or several related emails.

Is a campaign brief the same as an email content calendar?

No. They serve different planning purposes. A content calendar organizes email content across a period of time, while a campaign brief defines the purpose and requirements of an individual campaign.

Should the subject line be included in the brief?

It can be included as a working field, but it does not have to be finalized before the rest of the campaign is planned. The campaign goal, audience, message, and CTA should provide the direction for the subject line.

Can a solo email marketer use a campaign brief?

Yes. A brief can be useful even when you are the only person working on the campaign. It gives you a quick reference before writing, building, and reviewing the email.

Conclusion

A good email campaign usually becomes easier to create when the important decisions are made before writing begins.

Your campaign brief does not need to be complicated. Define the goal, audience, main message, content, CTA, tone, timing, and measurement approach.

Once those decisions are clear, you have a practical foundation for writing and producing the email.

The key is to use the brief as a planning tool rather than paperwork. It should help you answer one simple question: What does this email need to accomplish for the reader and the business?
