How to Write a Product Reorder Reminder Email for Subscription Customers

Customers who subscribe to recurring deliveries have already chosen a repeat-purchase arrangement. A useful reorder reminder helps them check an upcoming shipment, confirm that the product and quantity still suit them, and make changes before the next order is processed. The aim is to prevent surprises and give customers control—not to ask them to place a second order for something already scheduled.

Quick answer: Send a reminder before the subscription's actual order-processing or renewal cutoff. Identify the product and expected next-order date, state whether a charge or shipment is scheduled only when your system confirms it, and provide working options to review, skip, pause, or manage the subscription where those options are supported. Stop or update the reminder if the customer changes or cancels the plan.

Purpose of a Reminder for Subscription Customers

A subscription reorder reminder is a pre-order or pre-shipment message for customers receiving a product on a recurring schedule. It gives them time to check whether they still want the next delivery, whether the quantity is correct, and whether their address or payment details need attention.

This can be useful for recurring deliveries of coffee, personal-care products, pet supplies, household essentials, office consumables, or other products supported by a subscription plan. Not every subscription system allows the same changes, so only describe actions the customer can actually take.

A strong reminder reduces uncertainty. It should make the next scheduled event understandable and show the customer where to manage it. Avoid framing the reminder as a fresh purchase that is required when the customer already has an active recurring order.

How It Differs from a General Reorder Reminder

A general reorder reminder is often based on a previous purchase and an estimate of when the product might be needed again. A subscription reminder should be based on the customer's actual plan schedule and the business's processing cutoff.

  • General reorder reminder: Suggests that a customer may want to buy an item again; the customer decides whether to start a new order.
  • Subscription reorder reminder: Explains an already scheduled recurring order and provides available controls before processing.
  • Subscription renewal reminder: Often concerns the continuation or renewal of a subscription service or term. That can involve a different decision and should not be confused with the next shipment of a recurring physical product.
  • Order confirmation: Confirms an order after the order system records it; it is not a pre-order reminder.

How to Choose the Timing

Send the reminder early enough for the customer to make a meaningful change, but close enough to the scheduled order that the date and details are still relevant. The right lead time depends on the subscription rules, processing cutoff, product category, and how long changes take to apply.

  • Use the actual next-order date: Pull it from the subscription record, not a guess based on when the customer first purchased.
  • Respect the cutoff: Explain the deadline for skipping, changing, or cancelling only when the system and policy support that deadline.
  • Account for time zones: Make deadlines understandable in the customer's relevant time zone where possible.
  • Check recent changes: Do not send outdated details if the customer has already skipped a shipment, changed the schedule, or cancelled.
  • Coordinate messages: Avoid sending several reminders that repeat the same information within a short period.
Important: Do not promise that a change will affect the next shipment unless it is made before the relevant cutoff and the subscription system confirms the change. Explain the actual terms in plain language.

What to Include

The reminder should focus on the next scheduled order and the controls the customer can use.

  • Product and quantity: Identify the next scheduled items clearly, including size or variant if relevant.
  • Scheduled date: State the expected processing or shipment date only if the data is current.
  • Price or charge information: Include the upcoming amount and currency when available and accurate; clarify that taxes, shipping, or discounts may alter the final total when applicable.
  • Change options: Link to the supported options, such as skip, change quantity, edit frequency, pause, or cancel.
  • Cutoff information: Explain when changes must be made to affect the upcoming order.
  • Help route: Provide a real support path for questions about billing or shipment timing.

Do not include payment-card details in the email. Link customers to a secure account page for sensitive account changes.

Subject Line Examples

  • Your next [Product Name] delivery is coming up
  • Review your upcoming subscription order
  • Check your next [Brand Name] shipment
  • Your recurring order is scheduled for [Date]
  • Need to update your next delivery?
  • A reminder about your upcoming subscription order
  • Review your quantity before your next shipment

Use a preheader to add practical context, such as “Check the scheduled date and update your order before the cutoff.” Avoid subject lines that imply a charge has already occurred if the next order has not been processed.

How to Structure the Email

1. Name the upcoming event

Tell customers that the email concerns their next scheduled subscription order. Avoid vague messages that make them wonder whether they need to buy the product again manually.

2. Show the relevant details

Present product, quantity, expected date, and estimated total if available. If your system cannot verify a field, leave it out or direct the customer to their secure account page rather than inventing a value.

3. Make the controls obvious

Use a primary link such as “Review Next Order.” On the account page, show the supported management actions. Do not label a button “Skip” if it merely opens a help page or starts an email to support.

4. Explain the deadline plainly

If there is a cutoff, state the date and time in a readable format. Tell customers what happens after it passes, and avoid implying that an action can still change an order once it has entered processing.

5. Keep the message neutral and helpful

Subscription reminders should support an informed decision, not create false urgency. Let the customer manage the plan without guilt or pressure.

Practical Examples

Example 1: Upcoming recurring delivery

Subject: Review your upcoming [Product Name] order

Hello [First Name],

Your next scheduled order for [Product Name] is currently planned for [Scheduled Date]. The current plan includes [Quantity / Variant].

Please review the details and make any available changes before [Verified Cutoff].

[Review Next Order]

If your plans have changed or you need help, visit [Subscription Help Link].

Thank you,
[Brand Name] Team

Example 2: Customer can skip or change quantity

Subject: Need to adjust your next delivery?

Hello [First Name],

Your next [Product Name] delivery is scheduled for [Scheduled Date]. If you need a different quantity or do not need this shipment, your subscription may offer options to update or skip it before the cutoff.

Review the options available for your plan here: [Subscription Management Link]. The page will show which changes can still apply to the upcoming order.

Thanks,
[Brand Name] Team

Example 3: Scheduled order with upcoming charge

Subject: Check the details for your next subscription order

Hello [First Name],

Your subscription is scheduled to create the next order on [Scheduled Date]. The current estimated total is [Amount and Currency], subject to the terms and final calculation shown in your account.

If you want to review the amount, address, or available subscription settings, visit [Secure Account Link] before [Verified Cutoff].

For billing questions, contact [Support Contact].

[Brand Name] Team

Reusable Email Template

Subject: Your next [Product Name] delivery is coming up

Preheader: Review your next scheduled order and available changes.

Hello [First Name],

This is a reminder that your next subscription order for [Product Name] is currently scheduled for [Scheduled Date].

Upcoming order summary
Product: [Product and Variant]
Quantity: [Quantity]
Estimated total: [Amount and Currency, if confirmed]
Scheduled processing date: [Date]

You can review your next order and see available options here:

[Review or Manage Subscription]

If you want to skip or change the order, please check the options and cutoff shown in your account. The available changes depend on your plan and processing status.

Need help? Visit [Support Link].

Thank you,
[Sender Name]
[Brand Name]

Replace every bracketed field before sending. Remove any option the subscription does not actually support, test the links, and make sure the scheduled date and any amount come from current subscription data.

Automation and Suppression Rules

A reliable workflow should react to the live subscription state, not only a fixed delay after the first purchase. A practical process is:

  1. Read the current subscription's next-order date, items, status, and supported management options.
  2. Schedule the reminder relative to the actual processing cutoff.
  3. Immediately before sending, recheck whether the plan is active and whether its date, products, quantity, or status changed.
  4. Suppress the message if the subscription was paused, skipped, cancelled, or otherwise made ineligible.
  5. Use an idempotent send record so retrying a failed workflow does not deliver duplicate reminders.
  6. Record which reminder was sent and stop follow-ups when the customer makes the relevant change.

Test edge cases such as multiple subscriptions, changed billing dates, a skipped cycle, cancelled plans, failed payment, and items that are no longer available. Make sure a reminder does not contradict a separate payment or order-status message.

What to Measure

Evaluate whether reminders help customers manage the upcoming shipment clearly. Useful measures include delivery success, clicks to the subscription management page, completed skips or changes before cutoff, support contacts about unexpected shipments, unsubscribe or complaint rates, and accidental duplicate sends.

Do not treat a high click rate as the sole success measure. A reminder that helps a customer skip an unneeded shipment may be useful even if it does not directly create a new sale. Compare the results with the customer experience and the purpose of the subscription program.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Guessing the schedule: Use the active subscription record rather than the customer's historical average alone.
  • Calling an already scheduled order a new purchase: Make clear that the message refers to a recurring order.
  • Showing an outdated date or total: Refresh subscription data immediately before sending.
  • Promising unsupported controls: Only offer skip, pause, change, or cancellation options that actually exist.
  • Hiding the processing cutoff: Customers need to understand when a change must be made.
  • Sending after cancellation or skip: Recheck the live status and suppress stale reminders.
  • Using pressure or false urgency: Provide accurate dates and neutral language.
  • Sending repetitive messages: Coordinate reminders with order, payment, shipping, and renewal communications.

Pre-Send Checklist

  • The subscription is active and eligible for a reminder.
  • The upcoming date and product details are current.
  • Any estimated charge is accurate and clearly labelled.
  • The cutoff time and consequences are explained correctly.
  • All management links lead to the promised actions.
  • Skipped, paused, cancelled, or changed subscriptions are suppressed.
  • Duplicate messages are prevented.
  • The message is mobile-friendly and easy to scan.
  • Support contact details and preference controls are available where appropriate.

Final Recommendation

A subscription reorder reminder should make the next scheduled delivery predictable and manageable. Use actual plan data, explain any cutoff clearly, and direct customers to the options their subscription genuinely supports. The goal is to help customers make an informed choice and prevent surprises—not to create unnecessary orders or additional pressure.
